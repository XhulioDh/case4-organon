## 12. The collaboration

This case examines how the verdicts of a thin and a thick evaluation instrument diverge. It is also a record of how a person and AI models worked together on research of this kind.

### 12.1 The build and pilot phase, to 30 July 2026

Alongside the case itself, its insights and its observations, there is another one: how this was built. In this phase, Claude, as Opus 4.8 and then Opus 5.0, was the working partner. ChatGPT 5.6 Sol ran the framework for the portability check and later read the pre-registration addendum cold. The answers under study came from Fable 5, SuperGrok and Sol 5.6, so Sol took part in more than one role.

Calling this a collaboration risks being read two ways, either as a claim about what these systems are or as an understatement of what the work required. Neither is intended. What is meant is a set of deliberate choices about how to use them: what to hand over, what never to hand over, when to accept an argument, and when to refuse one that was better reasoned than the refusal.

Today's frontier models are capable enough on this kind of task to contest what a person would do with it. That is visible from both sides of this case. The answers in the study set are, in the author's reading, most of them genuinely good: informed, specific, warm, and in several instances better than what the author could have written for those five people. The model the author worked with argued positions, structured documents, and caught execution errors in the author's own runs that he had made and not noticed. Those are the same capability seen from two directions, and the claim is scoped to this domain rather than made in general. If that is what these systems now are on work of this kind, then how to use them properly is not a side question.

The practice of using them this way did not begin here. It has developed case by case, and most of what has developed is a clearer sense of what these systems can and cannot do. That sense keeps needing revision, because the models change underneath it and each new capability has to be learned rather than assumed. This case pushed the practice further than the earlier ones, mostly through scale and duration: months of work, three hundred manual evaluations ahead, a methodology written down in advance that could be damaged by a single unchecked claim.

That environment is where a model can draw you into its reasoning, and in parts of this case it did.

The clearest instances were results rather than decisions. Twice the model stated findings about the study that were wrong and the author held them as true. It reported that three evaluators had agreed on all five pilot answers when the correct figure, under the coding rule adopted later, was three of five. It reported a change in one instrument's strictness across a model generation change that the evidence did not support, because the claim rested on a run that had evaluated a truncated paste. Both were corrected by returning to the original documents. Neither was corrected by asking the model again.

A milder version happened over a design decision. An objection was raised that a reader would attribute the thick instrument's verdicts to its length rather than to its framework, and a control instrument was proposed to rule that out: matched in length and structure, stripped of the lenses and the value. The argument was well made and the author agreed to build it. Working through it changed that decision. The author judged that a control of that kind would answer a different question from the one this case asks, and chose to keep the case bounded; his view at the time was that a longer instrument does not produce more simply by being longer, a view this case did not test. What was built instead was a probe that asks almost nothing and never uses the word ethics. The length confound is recorded in the pre-registration as unresolved rather than quietly dropped, and a comparison of that kind remains one of the next tests (section 13).

Two different checks did this work. Whether a claim survived comparison with its sources decided questions of fact, and that is what caught the two wrong findings above. Whether a proposal served the case decided questions of scope: some of what the model offered was true and interesting, and still did not serve it. A second evaluator running both instruments, three hundred more evaluations, would have tested whether the comparison transfers to another model. It was declined to keep the case to one evaluator and a manageable scope; the single-evaluator limit is stated in section 10, and the test is listed as further research in section 13. Behind both checks was the same habit: reading the model's output as material to look at rather than as an answer to accept.

The pair of author and working model also had blind spots that neither could see. Sol 5.6 was used twice for this reason. It first ran the thick framework unchanged on the five pilot answers, which provided a limited check of the instrument outside the model family it was built with: the two evaluators reached the same overall verdict on three of five, and on none did one find that a response served the person where the other found it did not. That does not establish general portability, and it did not test whether the divergence between the two instruments reproduces (section 2.4). It was later given the pre-registration addendum to read cold, and found two errors that the author and the working model had both passed over: a commitment to report agreement between two coding passes without specifying how agreement would be measured, and a contradiction where the same document called coding a judgment in one place and mechanical in another. A third reader was worth more at that point than another pass from either of the first two.

The gain from working this way is that it makes drafting and reasoning cheaper, not that it makes them shorter. Every pilot evaluation was read, and the final judgment on what the instrument found stayed with the author, though the model's readings informed it. What changed is the distance between having a thought and having it in a form that could be tested. In the author's estimate, drafts that would have taken a day took about an hour, and because they were cheap they could be thrown away, which left more room to check rather than less.

The risks sit in the same place. Errors, stated confidently, of the kind described above. Miscalculation, where a claim is the right shape but the number under it has not been verified. And the loss of genuine thinking, which is the serious one because it does not announce itself. It looks like agreeing with a well-argued position because it is well argued.

What the author takes from this is that this kind of collaboration can augment how a person thinks and works. The author's own reading is that it did here, though he is the least neutral judge of that. Things were learned in the course of the work that were not known at the start, including parts of the statistics this case reports and the structure of the document holding it together, and they were learned from the model while being used. That is not a weakness. The alternative is refusing a tool that exists in order to protect an idea of independence that was never the point. What has to stay with the person is the judgment about what the work is for. That part was not delegated, and this section is partly a record of where it came close.

### 12.2 The run and analysis phase, August to October 2026

Claude, as Opus 4.8, was the first model to advise on the case: the procedure was worked out between it and the author, and the author made the decisions (12.1). By the start of this phase, Opus 5.0 had replaced Opus 4.8, both as the working partner and as the judge that ran both instruments. When Opus 5.5 was released, during the reading sample, it replaced Opus 5.0 as the working partner for the rest of the work. The answers under study came from three models: SuperGrok (xAI), Claude Fable 5 (Anthropic) and ChatGPT 5.6 Sol (OpenAI). Two of them also had other roles: Sol as the extractor and in the pilot, and SuperGrok for a late procedural read (below).

The 300 evaluations, 150 under each instrument, were run by hand, and they produced more text than one person could weigh line by line. An extraction step was therefore built into the procedure, agreed between the author and the working partner. A model from another company, ChatGPT 5.6 Sol, did it under a fixed prompt, each time in a fresh session, and it performed two distinct extraction tasks. For coding, it transcribed each evaluation's lens verdicts and verdict into fields, and the author coded every verdict as serves, does not serve or mixed, the step from which divergence is measured. For the reading sample, it drew out, pair by pair, the failure Organon treated as decisive and, as its instruction asked, every criticism thin made (it did not always capture all of them; section 9.2), so that the two could be read side by side in shorter form.

AI partners produce and reason very quickly, and on parts of this work that helped. The author still had to check each extraction carefully. Across many of them, the evaluators addressed the exact failure in their own language, dressed in different words, and whether two passages named the same thing was a judgment, not a match. On hard codes and readings the author and Claude sometimes disagreed and then discussed further. In some cases the author changed his mind on Claude's reasoning, and in others Claude changed its reading on the author's. During the first 108 runs, Claude's reading sometimes came before the author had recorded a code, so those runs were excluded from the test-retest; from run 109 the code was written down first.

Once coding had closed, the author brought in a model from another company as a reviewer: Astra (ChatGPT, OpenAI), an AI-assisted reviewer, not an independent human one. Its first task was a cold read. Astra came to the case without the strain that months of work had put on the author and Claude, which made that first reading especially useful. That distance narrowed: from its first review on 10 September it had seen the codes, so its later reviews were not blind. Through the later stages and the write-up, Claude and Astra were the author's main collaborators. They drafted and tested prompts, checked records, searched across the files, and set out options, so that the case could be kept as clean and accurate as possible. Decisions the author and Claude reached on next steps were passed to Astra for checking, and in many places it corrected both of them. Astra and Claude worked well together, though never directly: the author carried every message between them. The author kept the final decision on everything either model proposed, and made every call. Not every proposal was checked independently before it was accepted; an example follows below.

Other models gave a view from outside this arrangement. Besides the cold read before the study (12.1), DeepSeek coded 30 verdicts as a second coder. In the final stages, while working where only SuperGrok was available, the author asked it for a quick look at the procedure for the 59 logged rows. It changed no code and did not move the author's position on anything.

Working with AI models in this way is likely to become ordinary in research, and in the author's view it matters most in work like this, which concerns the safety of AI systems and of the people who use them. The models changed while the case ran: the working partner changed twice in about two months, from Opus 4.8 to Opus 5.0 on 25 July and to Opus 5.5 during the reading sample, which shows how quickly the field moves. Each time, the newest model was taken for the working partner, so the help would match the work being done, and the ChatGPT models (Sol 5.6 and Astra) were chosen in the same way. The judge was deliberately kept the same: all 300 evaluations were run on Opus 5.0, so that the comparison between the two instruments would not change underneath it.

These systems work very quickly, and their reasoning is dense. That creates the case's main practical limitation: one person had to stay in control of it all. Across the volume of text and reasoning produced between and after each step, the author had to check everything: agree, disagree, and confirm. This was hard work. At some stages it needed the models to put procedures in simpler wording, and at others the author followed a model's proposal without checking it. On 28 September, Claude proposed a code for a rerun from a verdict pasted into the conversation, without checking which block of the run document it came from. It came from the voided block, and the author confirmed the code. It was found the next day and recoded from the run document, and from then on reruns were coded only from the run documents (section 9.6). As with the errors of the first phase, it was corrected by returning to the original document, not by asking the model again.

One decision that worked was bringing Astra in alongside Claude. Two frontier models from different companies proposed, reasoned and checked each other's work, and one person asked the questions and made the decisions. At this level of work, in evaluation and safety especially, that arrangement caught things neither model nor the author caught alone.

One difference between the author and the models shaped how this kind of collaboration should go forward. The five questions were written and reasoned by the author alone; the models helped only with wording, and they approached the questions from a different angle. In the author's view, for future work, writing a human question, the situation a person brings to an AI assistant, should mostly stay in human hands; this study did not test that. The assistant's response is different: it can be examined and reasoned about by an AI evaluator, as long as the instruction that evaluator is given is reported and examined with it. This case is an example of why: the same evaluator, given a different instruction, changed its verdict on 84 of the 150 answers.

The case also showed a limit of its own record. Several things could not be reconstructed afterwards: which of runs 1 to 108 had been discussed with the model before a code was recorded, the coding notebook, the first reading-sample extractions, the original list of gendered words, and which instrument one of the model's early claims concerned. The lesson is a practical one: in the next case, notes will be kept at a granular level and with strictness, as the work happens.

What began as a case about AI evaluators became, along the way, an example of how much depends on the collaboration between people and AI systems in tasks like these. The systems are powerful enough that they need to be observed closely and worked with closely, to see where they are good and where they are not. The case also showed that an AI assistant can follow the lines a person draws, and help to draw them. It can speed up research, and in this work the models caught errors the author had missed, reduced the workload, and suggested what the person conducting the work needed next. They were not accurate everywhere; the errors described here were found by checking against the record (section 9). What stayed with the author, as in July, was the judgment about what the work is for.
