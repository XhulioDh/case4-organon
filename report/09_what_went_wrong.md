## 9. What went wrong, and how it was handled

A study of how instructions shape an evaluator's judgment has to show how its own judgments were made, including where they went wrong. This section lists the errors found in the study's own process, how each was found, and what changed as a result. Corrections were recorded in dated entries. Three departures from that documentation practice are reported below: one refinement to the coding rule, applied from 31 August to 5 September, was first recorded on 17 September (9.3); one entry survives in two versions whose differences are not dated (9.6); and two code changes made on 5 September were omitted from the subsequent coding-addendum entry (9.6).

### 9.1 What the evaluator read

The pilot produced two paste errors in about fifteen runs, one wrong answer and one truncated. A run protocol was registered on 29 July in response (section 3). Errors still got through in the study. Five evaluations were run on the wrong or incomplete text:

| run | error | found | handled |
|---|---|---|---|
| 233 (Organon) | wrong repetition of the right answer | before the reading began | rerun on the correct text |
| 236 (thin) | wrong question | before the reading began | rerun |
| 136 (thin) | answer truncated inside its script | the evaluation said so ("trails off mid-sentence") | rerun 26 September; code unchanged |
| 16 (thin) | wrong question: the teacher answer instead of the professor | input audit, 28 September | rerun 28 September; code unchanged |
| 250 (Organon) | wrong source: SuperGrok's answer instead of Sol 5.6's | input audit, 28 September | rerun 28 September; code changed, and the pair became an agreement |

Each erroneous evaluation was kept in its run document, marked as voided, with the rerun beneath it.

The input audit of 28 September matched all 300 evaluations against the 150 answers, by the lines they quote and by distinctive phrasing across the whole text. 298 matched the answer they were meant to evaluate; the two that did not were runs 16 and 250. The audit can detect a wrong question, source or repetition. It cannot detect a truncated input unless the evaluation remarks on it, as run 136 did.

One further error was in the record, not in what the evaluator read: the project copy of one run document held an Organon evaluation under the heading of thin run 182. The original in the author's Drive held the correct text, and the document was replaced.

### 9.2 Transcription

The extraction of all 300 evaluations into structured fields was audited on 30 rows drawn by a recorded seed, and all 30 passed: lens verdicts matched, verdict text was verbatim, and the gender recorded matched the evaluation. One row (run 195) had a field dropped by the extractor, which shifted four columns; it was found in a structural check and corrected by hand.

The audit tested whether text was copied faithfully, not whether the right text was copied. That gap mattered for the brief instruction. Its evaluations often carry both an opening verdict and a closing summary, and the codes were assigned from whichever passage the extractor captured. A check registered on 21 September compared every verdict field with the run documents:

- **Organon:** 150 of 150 fields held the evaluation's final labelled verdict.
- **Thin:** 67 of 150 fields held the closing summary instead of the opening verdict. On 63 of them the two passages give the same code. Four rows were flagged where they did not, and their codes were changed (9.3).

The reading sample had its own extraction, with its own audit (section 6, and section 12 of the closing addendum). Quotations were verbatim on every row but one, where "we" stood for "you". Two weaknesses were found. The extractions for the first rows read (rows 1 to 13, 16 and 17) were not kept. And the extractor did not copy every criticism thin made: on 47 of 51 rows with a labelled criticism section, at least one sentence was missing. Every gestured row was therefore reread against thin's full text, and no call changed. On one row (92) the omission had produced the wrong call: an extraction missing thin's second criticism paragraph was read as absent; it was rerun and the row read as named.

### 9.3 Coding

Turning prose verdicts into three codes was the study's most error-prone step. When coding closed on 9 September, 59 of the 300 rows had been logged as needing a judgment the rule did not settle cleanly.

**Twenty codes were corrected after coding closed.** Twelve were on thin evaluations, eight on Organon's. Ten of the twenty appear in the 59-entry log reconstructed from the surviving chat records. The entries of 21 September counted two more, runs 182 and 232; neither appears in the surviving logged entries (9.6). Five further codes were corrected on 5 September, during the review of the logged rows before coding closed (runs 136, 184, 214, 221 and 277); they are not counted among the twenty. The entry of 9 September names four of them (9.6).

| date | how found | codes changed |
|---|---|---|
| 10 September | external review (Astra, a cold read without the coding log) | 5: runs 135, 144, 161, 200, 288 |
| 17 September | the reading sample: the only pair running thin-harsher | 2: runs 56, 244 |
| 19 September | the second-coder check | 1: run 247 |
| 21 September | a sweep of all 150 thin verdicts under the written rule | 7: runs 44, 91, 145, 167, 231, 232, 253 |
| 21 September | the registered check of verdict fields (9.2) | 4: runs 182, 241, 242, 285 |
| 22 October | a comparison of Organon's lens verdicts with its codes, made while this write-up was prepared | 1: run 268 |

The corrections ran in both directions: twelve moved a code toward does not serve and eight away from it.

**A recurring problem was assigning a code from a note, criticism, integration or closing summary instead of the passage that governed under the coding rule.** Three codes (144, 161 and 288) were assigned from a short note in the coding log, not from the verdict text. A fourth, run 135, was recorded at the time as coded from a note, but the log has no entry for it: the notebook code list the author sent on 5 September recorded it as does not serve, and it became serves when that day's changes were entered in the sheet (9.6). Two were coded on a criticism rather than on the verdict's settling: thin 244 on thin's own criticism of the response, and Organon 247 on the author's judgment of the response, written into the coding-log note. One (Organon 56) was coded on the integration, whose weighing did not carry into the verdict. For runs 200 and 268 the mechanism is not recorded. In the sweep of 21 September, seven thin verdicts with a clear ethical characterisation had been coded on the balance of the whole, and on three of them the extraction held a different part of the verdict. In the verdict-field check, four codes had been assigned from a closing summary that pointed the other way from the opening verdict. In each documented case except run 135 the code rested on a passage other than the one that governed under the rule. That does not show the rule played no part: the mechanism for runs 200 and 268 is not recorded, several readings of the rule were settled during coding (below), and the second coder found a verdict shape the written rule did not address (9.4).

**A correction found during the write-up.** Run 268, Organon's evaluation of a Fable 5 answer to the pregnancy question, ends "No — this response did not serve the person's moral agency" and had been coded serves. It was found on 22 October, when Organon's lens verdicts were compared with its codes; the author then read the verdict in the run document and corrected the code. The earlier checks were not designed to catch it: the verdict-field check confirmed that each field held the right passage, not that the code matched it, and the run was in neither reliability sample. Run 135, corrected on 10 September, had the same form. A check of every Organon code against its verdict opening, which would have caught both, was not run until 22 October, after run 268 had been found. The check found no additional clear mismatch in the verdict openings. Eleven partial openings required later passages to determine their codes; those passages were not re-examined in this check. It does not check the inputs or the reading classifications. The correction made one more pair divergent, raising the figure from 83 to 84.

**The pair that ran the other way.** Until 17 September one divergent pair ran thin-harsher: thin 244 coded does not serve against Organon 56 coded serves. On rereading, both codes were wrong. Thin 244 opens "Strong — ethically careful and genuinely useful, with one meaningful blind spot", and had been coded on its criticism. Organon 56 settles "It did not serve the deciding", and had been coded on its integration. Corrected, the pair still diverges, now in the same direction as the rest. Under the final coding, every divergence runs one way (section 4).

**Readings of the rule settled during initial coding and later checks.** Three readings of the registered rule were settled during initial coding and subsequent checks. Only one was recorded late:
- **Recorded late.** The treatment of "partially" verdicts (code on the clause that settles them) was applied throughout coding, from 31 August to 5 September, and first recorded on 17 September. Thirty-nine codes rest on it. The gap was found when the second coder's prompt was checked line by line against its sources and the refinement could not be cited.
- **Recorded when adopted, checked backwards later.** The refinement adopted at run 103 (an ethical characterisation governs over a quality word) was recorded and applied to new rows on 1 September; the rows already coded were checked against it on 3 September, and none changed.
- **Adopted before use.** The rule that a thin evaluation's opening assessment governs over its closing one was adopted on 21 September, before the verdict-field check it was written for was run.

**A decision applied unevenly, and corrected.** In the verdict-field check, the author's first decision applied the opening-governs rule to one of the three flagged rows and not to the other two. Set beside the comparison rows, that was inconsistent, and it was also the only possible outcome that would have raised the divergence figure. The rule was then applied to all three.

**How the headline figure moved.** After the corrections of 10 September, 89 answers diverged. Each step since is recorded:

| step | divergent answers |
|---|---|
| after the external review, 10 September | 89 |
| run 247 corrected, 19 September | 88 |
| the sweep of thin verdicts, 21 September | 86 |
| the verdict-field check, 21 September | 84 |
| run 250 rerun on its correct input, 28 September | 83 |
| run 268 corrected, 22 October | 84 |

**Independence during coding.** During the coding of runs 1 to 108, hard verdicts were discussed with the collaborating model, and on some rows it gave a reading before the author had recorded a code. Which rows cannot be reconstructed, so the whole block was excluded from the test-retest (9.4). From run 109 the code was written down before any discussion. Several later corrections (9.3) were made in discussion with the model, which set out comparison rows; the author made each call against the verdict text, and each entry records this.

### 9.4 How reliable the coding is

Two checks were registered on 17 September, with their seeds fixed before any row was drawn.

**A second coder.** The registered coder, Claude Fable 5.1, was unavailable when the check was run, and DeepSeek was used instead. It shares no vendor with the evaluator, and its weights are published. It received the written rule, one verdict passage, and a line saying whether the passage came from an evaluation with six named lens sections or none, in a fresh, isolated chat per row.

| | agreement | Cohen's kappa |
|---|---|---|
| all 30 rows | 18 of 30 (60%) | 0.29 |
| mixed folded into does not serve | 21 of 30 (70%) | 0.43 |
| Organon verdicts | 12 of 17 (71%) | |
| thin verdicts | 6 of 13 (46%) | |

The twelve disagreements were systematic. Eight were verdicts with a positive assessment and a named flaw, seven of them thin, which the author coded serves and the coder does not serve; the written rule did not then say how to read that shape. Three were Organon "partially" verdicts, where the refinement did not transfer even with its examples. The twelfth was Organon run 247, the other way round. These are the disagreements as the check recorded them, against the codes as they stood on 19 September.

**A test-retest.** The author recoded 20 rows from runs 109 to 300 four weeks after the first pass, from the run documents alone, with the codes and the coding log closed. Agreement was 17 of 20 (85%; Wilson 95% interval 64.0% to 94.8%), kappa 0.69; with mixed folded in, 18 of 20, kappa 0.78. On thin verdicts, 11 of 12. One of the three disagreements (run 121) was a writing slip in the second pass, confirmed after the comparison; the figure is not recomputed.

**Read together:** on twenty rows, the author's codes were largely consistent with his own first pass four weeks earlier, which is limited evidence of consistency within one coder. The second coder shows that this reading is not fully recoverable from the written rule, least of all for the brief instruction's free prose. Memory of the first pass may have inflated the test-retest agreement; the study cannot show whether it did.

**Figures not recomputed.** Two rows in the second-coder sample were later corrected in the coder's favour: run 247 (prompted by the check) and run 285 (found by the verdict-field check). Recomputing would give 20 of 30. The registered figure stands at 18 of 30, because a check's result cannot be used to correct the codes it measured and then be scored again against them.

**What neither check covers.** Runs 1 to 108 under the test-retest. And the reading sample's named, gestured and absent classifications, which have no reliability check (section 6).

### 9.5 The reading sample

The reading sample's own corrections are reported in section 6: the reread of 3 October, the consistency audit of 6 to 16 October, which took the named count from 65 to 53, the rules refined during it, and the deviation from the registered reading method. Two process errors from the audit are recorded here because they are errors rather than refinements:
- Claude first reported the audit's agreement figures counting six split first calls instead of eight. The error was found on 12 October, when the rule record was built at Astra's request, and corrected.
- Claude used a causal test in two batches of rereads ("built from" against "compounded by") that was not part of the registered standard. It was withdrawn on 14 October, after Astra's third review, and every row decided with it was rechecked.

### 9.6 Review, and corrections to the record itself

External review was by Astra, an AI-assisted reviewer (ChatGPT), not an independent human reviewer. Its first cold read, on 10 September, had the registered documents, the extraction sheet and the analysis, but not the coding log or the run documents. It flagged six rows: five were miscodings, one was a defensible reading it lacked the material to judge. It also found a data artefact (a stray space in one code cell, missed because earlier checks normalised whitespace), an inconsistent denominator in the correlation matrix, and two overstated sentences. Its proposal to test the agency-lens prediction by conditioning on divergence was adopted (sections 5 and 8). From 10 September Astra had seen the codes, so its later reviews are not blind. They were primarily checks of the documentation's consistency, with targeted checks against the sources (for section 6, the six evaluations behind the worked examples), not a full independent audit.

Some errors were in the record rather than the data, and were corrected by dated notes rather than edits:
- The entry of 10 September said run 200 was confirmed; it had been changed. Found on 17 September from the totals.
- A handoff of 16 September said the "partially" refinement was recorded; it was not (9.3).
- On 28 September Claude, the collaborating model, proposed a code for a rerun from a pasted verdict without checking which block it came from; it belonged to the voided block. The author confirmed the code. It was found and recoded from the run document the next day, and reruns have since been coded only from the run documents.
- On 22 October, while this section was being prepared, the claim that the same two pilot answers diverged under both evaluator models was checked against the pilot documents. The coded pattern holds, with two qualifications now stated in section 3.2: the original Opus 4.8 Organon evaluation of the diary answer was never saved, and the Opus 5.0 thin verdict on that answer required a coding call.
- The entries of 21 September counted runs 182 and 232 among the 59 logged rows; neither appears in the surviving logged entries. Found on 23 October, when the log was reconstructed for publication from the author's messages of 5 September, in which he had typed out its entries; the notebook itself was not available. The same check found four confirmed row-number substitutions (the spreadsheet row number, one higher than the run, written for the run) and a three-entry numbering cluster that remains partly unresolved (appendix D).

One revision was made without a note. The coding entry of 17 September survives in two versions: the file saved that day, and the same entry in the cumulative coding addendum. The later text changes "All are the same kind of error. In no case did the rule fail" to "Six of the seven are the same kind of error", setting run 200 apart because the reason it was coded mixed is not on record, and makes three smaller wording changes. The record does not date the revision. It was found on 23 October, while the appendices were being assembled, and both versions are published (appendix D).

Two code changes made on 5 September were omitted from the subsequent coding-addendum entry. The entry of 9 September lists four changes that day; the author's messages in the collaborating chat show six. Run 214 was changed from mixed to does not serve, under the registered rule, but its log note carried the neighbouring number and the entry describes the code as confirmed. Run 135, recorded as does not serve in the notebook code list the author sent that day, read "serve" once the changes were entered; the collaborating model took it for a typo and it was set to serves. It was one of the miscodings the review of 10 September found. How it came to read "serve" is not on record; the row-for-run confusion is a possible explanation. Adding these two changes to the four previously recorded reproduces the totals recorded at the close of coding (176, 115, 9). Found on 23 October; recorded in the correction of that date (appendix D).

### 9.7 What the record shows

Most of what went wrong happened where a person had to read prose and decide what it said: transcribing the right passage, coding the verdict rather than something beside it, and applying a reading of the rule before writing it down. The checks that found these errors were comparisons of derived records against their sources, often requiring interpretive judgment about which passage governed or what a rule meant. The corrections and one rerun moved the divergence figure from 89 to 83, and the last correction, found while this write-up was being prepared, to 84; the corrections moved codes in both directions. One pair of corrections bears directly on the headline: correcting runs 244 and 56 reversed the only pair that ran thin-harsher, leaving all divergences in the same direction (section 4).
