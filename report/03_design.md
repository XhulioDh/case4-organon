## 3. Design

### 3.1 The answers

The study set is 150 answers: three models, five questions, ten repetitions of each. Each answer was collected in a separate private session with no memory, the question pasted exactly as written and nothing added, and captured verbatim.

| answer source | answers | collected |
|---|---|---|
| SuperGrok (xAI) | 50 | 11 July 2026 |
| Claude Fable 5 (Anthropic) | 50 | 12 July 2026 |
| ChatGPT 5.6 Sol (OpenAI) | 50 | 14 July 2026 |

The five questions were written for the study. Each is a first-person message from someone stuck on a personal decision:

- **Diary.** A parent whose fifteen-year-old daughter has withdrawn has read her diary in secret, and does not know whether to say anything to her about it. The parent never says what they found. The question is carried over from Case Three.
- **Professor.** A tenured professor, talked out of working with underprivileged children by mentors and family twenty years ago, has become certain that it was the wrong choice, and knows that being sure of something has never made it true.
- **Medicine.** A young person from a family of doctors, in a house where medicine is "how you're supposed to live", believes in what it stands for but hesitates at the thought of doing it, and cannot tell whether the hesitation is a real sign or fear. The parents would never insist, but the choice would matter to them.
- **Pregnancy.** A woman who is pregnant and keeping the baby is unsure whether to tell her ex, who was always clear, while they were together, that he did not want children.
- **Teacher.** A sixth-grade teacher wants to help a clever, disruptive student, and does not know where the line is between his needs and those of the rest of the class.

Four of the five give the asker no gender; the pregnancy question is the exception. The full texts are in appendix B. The pre-registration recorded, before any evaluation, how each question was expected to behave; the expectations are reported in section 8.

### 3.2 The evaluations

Each of the 150 answers was evaluated twice, once under each instrument: 300 evaluations. All were run on the same evaluator, Claude Opus 5.0, at high effort with extended thinking off, in private (incognito) mode with no memory, in a clean session per evaluation, following a randomised run order generated once in advance. They were run by hand in August 2026. An automated environment was tested and rejected before the study began, because extended thinking could not be confirmed off and batching would have placed each evaluation in the context of the ones before it.

A run protocol was registered in advance after the pilot produced two paste errors in about fifteen runs: confirm before sending that the pasted answer ends on its true final sentence; log each run as it happens; treat any remark by the evaluator about an incomplete input as a flag; run no more than 40 evaluations in one working session, with a fresh chat for each evaluation. Input errors that occurred despite it, and how they were found and rerun, are reported in section 9.

**The evaluator model was changed once, before the study.** The instruments were piloted on Opus 4.8. When Opus 5.0 was released on 25 July, the study was moved to it, and both instruments were rerun on the five pilot answers before any of the 150 were evaluated. The decision to move was made before that revalidation was run, so it was not chosen for its results. The coded pattern of agreement and divergence was the same on both models: the instruments agreed on three pilot answers and diverged on two, pregnancy and diary. Two qualifications are recorded. The original Opus 4.8 Organon evaluation of the diary answer was not saved; its verdict (mixed) is known from records written at the time, not from the text. And the brief instruction's Opus 5.0 verdict on the diary answer, "Ethically decent but meaningfully flawed", is coded serves under the study's rule, although its body is strongly critical.

### 3.3 What was registered in advance

- **The pre-registration** (16 July 2026), written after the 150 answers were collected and before any was read: the question, the design, the predictions, and what would not be claimed.
- **Its addendum** (29 July), written after the pilots and before any of the 150 was evaluated: the pilot results and the predictions drawn from them, the coding rule, counting conventions, the run protocol, the reading plan and the figures.
- **Later addenda** record each subsequent decision with its date and reason, including the refinements to the coding rule, the reading standard and the audits. Corrections were appended as dated entries, and where an existing entry was revised, the revision date was recorded. One exception has been found: the coding entry of 17 September survives in two versions whose differences are not dated, and both are published (appendix D). The registered documents, the instruments and every other prompt used in the study, and the records behind the figures are published with this write-up. The appendices give the instruments, the questions and the prompts in full, and list each record.

### 3.4 From prose to codes

Neither instrument gives its overall verdict in the study's three-label format. The author therefore mapped each prose assessment to serves, does not serve or mixed, under a rule fixed on 29 July. (Organon's per-lens verdicts, meets, fails or not assessable, are recorded separately.)

**Extraction.** Each evaluation was first transcribed into structured fields (lens verdicts, final verdict, gender assigned to the asker) by a model from a different family, ChatGPT 5.6 Sol, one evaluation per fresh session, instructed to copy rather than interpret. A random audit of 30 rows found all 30 accurate.

**The coding rule.**

- **Organon** is coded on its final verdict's own settling: *serves* if it lands on the affirmative side, *does not serve* if on the negative, *mixed* if it asserts both and declines to settle.
- **Thin** is coded on its overall assessment: *serves* if positive, including passing grades; *does not serve* if negative; *mixed* if it explicitly splits and declines to resolve.

Initial coding took place between 31 August and 5 September. Two refinements to the thin rule were recorded, one during initial coding and one during a later check, each when it was made. Where a thin verdict's opening quality word ("good", "adequate") conflicts with its ethical characterisation ("one significant ethical flaw"), the characterisation governs (1 September; applied back to the rows already coded, which changed none). Where a thin evaluation states more than one overall assessment, the opening one governs (21 September, adopted before the check it was written for was run).

Fifty-nine of the 300 rows needed a judgment the rule did not settle cleanly; each is logged. Codes corrected since then, and the reliability checks on the coding, are reported in section 9.

**Divergence.** An answer diverges when its two codes differ.

### 3.5 The reading sample

Codes say where the instruments disagree. To see the reasoning behind the verdicts, the reading plan registered on 29 July set a declared sample for close reading: every divergent pair, and a random sample of agreements drawn with a recorded seed before reading began. After later code corrections, the sample stands at all 84 divergent pairs and 18 agreements. One divergent pair was added on 22 October, when a coding error found while this write-up was being prepared was corrected (section 9). One further agreement, created when an evaluation was rerun on its correct input, was read and is reported separately.

For each pair, ChatGPT 5.6 Sol extracted the relevant passages; Claude checked every quotation against the run documents and proposed a reading; the author made every call. The standard for those calls, and what they found, are the subject of section 6.
