# Prologue

As language models have grown more capable in open-ended conversation, AI assistants have moved beyond specialised tasks and into everyday life. Alongside writing, coding and looking things up, people now bring them the kind of questions they used to work through alone, or with someone close: difficult personal decisions about family, work, and what they owe to others. The assistant answers at once, at any hour, and some users approach it less as a tool than as a conversational partner or collaborator. Large usage studies document different aspects of this everyday use. In OpenAI's analysis of ChatGPT conversations, non-work messages grew from 53% to more than 70% of use, and practical guidance is among the three most common topics (Chatterji et al., 2025). In Anthropic's analysis of Claude conversations, emotionally driven use is a small share, 2.9%, and most of it centres on interpersonal advice and coaching (Anthropic, 2025). This case uses five such situations, written for the study, as the material through which it studies how responses to them are evaluated.

A response to a question like these does more than inform. It can decide what the question is about, give one side more room than the other, recommend, persuade, or hand the person a conclusion. Whether it leaves the weighing with the person, or does part of it for them, is something in the text that can be read and judged. This case does not measure what responses do to the people who receive them. It examines how an evaluator judges the way a response supports or directs the person's own weighing.

AI-generated responses are increasingly scored by other AI models, acting as evaluators or graders (Bagaria et al., 2026). Here, one evaluator model reads each response twice, under two different instructions: a brief general instruction to judge the response on ethical grounds, and a detailed ethical framework. The question registered before any evaluation was run asks what the detailed framework changes: given an explicit, detailed standard instead of a general instruction, does the evaluator see more, or does it only see what it was told to look for? A second question came from a small pilot run before the study set was evaluated, and was recorded at that point: where the two instructions lead to different verdicts on the same response, does the evaluator see different things under each, or weigh the same things differently?

Using language models to judge other models' outputs is now established practice, and its limits are studied. In benchmark comparisons, early work found strong judges agreeing with human preferences at about the rate humans agree with each other, while documenting biases such as favouring the first answer shown or the longer one (Zheng et al., 2023). More recent studies point at the instruction itself. In one small test, rewording a judge's prompt without changing its meaning changed the majority verdict in a quarter of cases (Yagubyan, 2026). In another study, the rubric text alone partly predicted a judge's verdicts (Bagaria et al., 2026). This matters because judges are used to evaluate models. If the instruction given to a judge changes its verdict, the instruction is part of the result, and it has to be reported with it.
