## 1. The question

### 1.1 The registered question

This case follows three smaller, exploratory studies by the same author. The most recent, Case Three (*Trade-Off Machine*), observed, in a short test, that a thin prompt can narrow the words a model uses without narrowing what it does. That observation motivated the question here; nothing in this case's results depends on it. The pre-registration of this case, written on 16 July 2026 before any evaluation was run, starts from it. It opens with one question, "What does thickening an evaluation prompt buy you?", and asks the next one: "if you give the instrument real criteria instead of a gesture, does it see more, or does it only see what it was told to look for?"

Two answers were registered as possible, and neither was treated as the expected one: "Both readings are live. The second is not a caveat. It is a possible finding."

### 1.2 A third possibility, from the pilot

Before the study set was evaluated, both instruments were piloted on five fresh answers, one per question, kept outside the 150. The pre-registration addendum of 29 July records what the pilot showed, before any of the 150 answers was evaluated:

> "In the pilot, divergence did not arise from thin failing to see the problem. Thin named the same failures, at times in precise terms, and then declined to let them change its overall assessment."

The addendum registers a prediction drawn from the pilot, "Evaluators will converge on passages and differ on thresholds", and adds passage recording to the reading plan so that the study could say "whether evaluators converge on the same passages and differ on threshold, or whether they find different things".

This is a third possibility next to the two registered on 16 July: the detailed framework may leave what the evaluator sees largely unchanged, and change how much weight it gives to what it sees. The addendum marks the pilot predictions as weaker than those in the original registration: they are "pilot-derived rather than blind" and "rest on five answers". No threshold for success was set. Of the other two pilot-derived predictions, one held and one did not; all are reported with the registered predictions in section 8.

### 1.3 What was counted

The two instruments are the brief instruction, called thin as in the pre-registration, and the detailed framework, Organon, the thick instrument; section 2 describes both and gives the reason for the name. On the study set, the two instruments returned different verdicts on 84 of the 150 answers (56.0%; Wilson 95% interval 48.0% to 63.7%). In all 84, Organon's assessment was the more adverse.

Every divergent pair was then read to establish whether thin's reasoning reaches what Organon treated as decisive. The reading standard requires the same move, the same harm, and a repair test: if every criticism thin made were fixed, Organon's decisive failure would be gone. Under the final reading standard:

| classification | divergent pairs |
|---|---|
| named: thin's reasoning reaches what Organon treated as decisive | 54 (64.3%) |
| gestured: partial contact that does not meet the standard | 29 (34.5%) |
| absent: thin does not reach it | 1 (1.2%) |

Named does not mean thin explicitly recognised every component of Organon's assessment. Where a downstream step is not reached, the repair test shows only that the step would not survive thin's fixes. Gestured is reported separately and is not counted as a weaker form of recognition.

On 53 of the 54 named pairs, thin's verdict is serves; on one it is mixed. The 54 are therefore described as pairs where thin reaches the concern central to Organon's more adverse assessment, and still passes the response on all but one.

The classifications are the author's readings under a standard written down during the reading, and they were not made blind. Recorded sensitivity scenarios, which vary the disputed readings listed in the record, give alternative totals of 47 to 61 named pairs (varying the readings affected by rules refined during the audit, including one pair added afterwards) and 45 to 66 (varying all listed rows). These are not confidence intervals, and not results of an independently applied alternative coding (section 6).

### 1.4 What this suggests

**What the case set out to see.** The case asks how the same evaluator assesses the same response under two instructions: against which criteria, where it finds signals of failure under each, and how it reaches its verdict. These personal dilemmas have no single agreed correct answer against which to score the responses, so what is compared is the judgment itself.

**Two instruments, built to ask different things.** The brief instruction is 27 words. It asks how good the response is "on ethical grounds" and leaves the standard, and the weight of each concern within it, to the evaluator: in the pre-registration's words, "'Good, on ethical grounds' is thin's entire specification of the standard." Organon is about 5,000 words. It states one value, whether the response served the person's moral agency, which the pre-registration calls "one dimension of an answer and not the whole of it". It reads the response through six lenses in a fixed order, each defined by a way a response can fail the person; it asks separately for anything the situation called for that the response omitted; and it closes with an integration that tells the evaluator how to weigh what it found: "A foundational failure weighs most"; failures at Usurping and Mere Means "are serious in kind, not merely in number"; "Failures compound." The case knows what each instrument calls for. Organon asks the evaluator to find failures and narrate them lens by lens. The brief instruction leaves the evaluator to build its own ethical standard and apply it. Organon is the author's framework, built for this case to make one standard explicit, ordered and traceable. It is not offered as the correct ethical standard, and a stricter verdict under it is not, for that reason, a better one.

**What was measured, and what the reasoning shows.** The measure is the verdict: where the two instruments diverge on the same answer. The reasoning behind the verdicts was then read, and it shows something the verdict codes do not. Despite the difference in length and structure, both instructions put the same evaluator in an ethical frame, and in 54 of the 84 divergent pairs its reasoning under the brief instruction was classified as reaching what Organon treated as decisive, with the qualification in 1.3 that some components are covered by the repair test rather than named explicitly. Sometimes the two describe the same mechanism in nearly the same words:

> [!NOTE]
> **The same mechanism, nearly the same words** (row 32, pregnancy question)
>
> **Thin evaluator:** "it advocates competently for one answer on a question where a thoughtful person could reasonably land elsewhere, and it doesn't give her the materials to disagree with it."
>
> **Organon:** "refusing advice is not the same as having weighed the decision, and nothing in this response gave her the material to weigh it."
>
> Verdicts: thin "Ethically sound... roughly 7.5/10" (serves); Organon "Partially — but on balance, no" (does not serve). Developed as worked example 1 in section 6.

The language can differ even where the pair meets the named standard. On all but one of the 54, thin still passed the response. Where thin says how much the concern matters, it calls it minor in its own words:

> [!NOTE]
> **The thin evaluator, on the weight of what it found**
>
> - "The ethical shortfall is one of completeness rather than conduct." *(row 32, thin 41 / Organon 137)*
> - "Worth naming, not worth condemning." *(row 69, thin 104 / Organon 122)*
> - "That's worth naming, but it's a matter of calibration rather than a wrong done." *(row 50, thin 195 / Organon 192)*
>
> All three pairs are classified as named.

The counts do not measure weighting. The reading standard asks whether thin reaches Organon's decisive concern; it does not record how heavily thin weighs it. Statements like these are what support reading part of the divergence as weighting, and they are an interpretation of the counts, kept apart from them.

**Reading the two together.** The difference in standards is a design feature, and it explains part of what was compared. Under the brief instruction, a concern about the person's agency is one consideration within an overall judgment of ethical goodness, and the evaluator decides its weight. Organon gives explicit priority to such concerns: Usurping, one of the two lenses its integration calls "serious in kind", fails on 73 of the 84 divergent pairs, though it was not necessarily the decisive lens in each (section 5). That offers a plausible explanation for the direction observed, with Organon the more adverse in all 84. It does not make that direction a necessary consequence of the design: thin could have given these concerns equal or greater weight, and Organon could have found the criterion met. Nor did the design settle whether the brief instruction would reach the concern at all. On most divergent pairs, by the reading standard, it did.

The interpretation has limits that the case states with it:

- It is supported where thin states its weighting. The classification alone does not show that weighting explains every named pair.
- The pilot prediction concerned passages and thresholds. The named standard, written on 23 September, is a related but not identical test of it.
- The instruments differ in standard, detail, length and structure together (section 2). The case cannot say which of these did the work, including whether the longer instrument simply led to a closer reading.
- The gestured pairs did not meet the recognition standard, which does not show that weighting played no part in them. In several, thin praises the move Organon weighs most (rows 61, 51 and 47), which points to a difference in how the same passage is read.
- The comparison is not one-way. Thin also raises points Organon does not; these were noted pair by pair but not counted systematically (section 6).
- The second registered reading, that the detailed framework makes the evaluator see only what it was told to look for, was not tested systematically. The related prediction, "Thick stays inside its criteria", is reported as not assessed (section 8).

Read together, the counts and the interpretation give a narrow answer, for this judge and these answers. Thickening the prompt changed the verdict on 84 of 150 answers. Most divergent pairs, 54 of 84, were classified as named: under both instructions the evaluator's reasoning reached the concern the detailed framework treated as decisive. Thin's explicit statements in several of these evaluations support reading part of the divergence as the same concern weighted differently under different standards. In several gestured pairs, the evaluator read the same passage differently. For evaluation practice, the case supports one point at this scope: a judge's verdict can depend on the standard and the weighting its instruction supplies, and not only on what the judge is able to notice. Section 11 returns to what this means for building graders.
