## 13. The open question

For one evaluator and 150 answers, this case gives a narrow answer to the question it registered. Replacing a brief ethical instruction with a detailed framework changed the verdict on 84 of the 150 answers, and in every case the detailed framework was the more adverse. On 54 of those 84, the brief instruction's reasoning was classified as reaching the concern the framework treated as decisive, and on 53 of them it still passed the response. Some of those evaluations state explicitly that the concern counts for little: "Worth naming, not worth condemning." Statements like these support reading part of the divergence as the same concern weighted differently under different standards.

That answer leaves a further question open. The two instructions differ in four things at once: the standard they set, the detail they give, their length, and their structure, including Organon's rules for weighing what is found. The design does not separate them, and the case cannot say which one did the work. The effort confound registered on 29 July, that a longer instruction may simply produce a closer reading, is unresolved (section 10).

Further research could test the contribution of these differences through controlled comparisons, run with the same evaluator on the same answers. The examples below are starting points. Each would need matched controls, and none isolates a single difference on its own:

- the brief instruction with Organon's value in place of "ethical grounds", which tests that particular wording of the standard;
- the lens names without the value, against a baseline specified in advance, since the names themselves carry a standard and some detail;
- Organon without its integration, the part that tells the evaluator how to weigh what it finds, which tests the contribution of that section, although removing it also shortens the instruction;
- a general ethical rubric of similar length and detail to Organon but without its value, which bears more directly on whether the divergence comes from a closer reading rather than from the framework. Lengthening the brief instruction with neutral text would test the effect of that padding, which could also distract the evaluator.

One further question belongs with these: whether the divergence reproduces on an evaluator outside the model family the framework was built with (section 10). These are suggestions for further research. This case does not undertake them.

Evaluating how model systems behave has to come with an understanding of how they are evaluated, and why: what a grader is asked to look for, and how it is told to weigh what it finds. This case shows, for one evaluator and one set of answers, how much a verdict can depend on that. How strongly these systems shape the reasoning of the people who use them is not something this case measures. Answering that will also require examining what the evaluations themselves measure.
